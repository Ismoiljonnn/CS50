At the heart of computer science lies problem-solving through algorithms. An algorithm is simply a finite, precise sequence of step-by-step instructions designed to solve a specific problem or perform a computation. Every software program, from a simple calculator to a massive AI model, follows a fundamental model: taking an input, passing it through an algorithm (logic/processing), and producing an output.

However, not all algorithms are created equal. Efficiency matters, especially when dealing with massive datasets. This is where the concept of algorithmic efficiency and Big O intuition comes into play. To demonstrate this, Professor David J. Malan famously uses a physical phone book analogy:

Linear Search: Imagine looking for a specific name by flipping through a phone book page by page, from the very beginning to the end. In the worst-case scenario, you have to check every single item. This is known as linear search, which has a time complexity of $O(n)$, meaning the time it takes grows directly in proportion to the number of items ($n$). If the phone book has 1,000 pages, you might have to look at all 1,000 pages.

Binary Search: Instead of checking one by one, a much smarter approach is the divide-and-conquer strategy. David demonstrates this by opening the phone book right in the middle, tearing it in half, and instantly throwing away the half where the name definitely isn't. By repeating this process—halving the remaining problem size at every single step-you narrow down the search exponentially. This is binary search, with a time complexity of $O(\log n)$. For a phone book with 1,000 pages, you can find the target name in just about 10 steps instead of 1,000!

Algorithms provide the logical blueprint for computers to transform inputs into outputs. Understanding efficiency reveals why some programs run instantly while others freeze up under heavy loads. By choosing smarter strategies like divide-and-conquer over brute-force linear checks, we drastically reduce the computational effort required to solve problems.
